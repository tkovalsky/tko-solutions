The team is busy, requests are waiting, and another coordinator looks like the obvious next hire. The useful question is what that person would actually spend the day doing.

If the work requires additional expertise or sustained handling capacity, hiring may be necessary. If it requires finding the same facts and asking the same authority questions repeatedly, first inspect the coordination problem.

## Follow the waiting work

Take a sample of requests from intake to completion. Record the active work, time waiting, missing input, owner, and reason for escalation. Include exceptions, not just the normal path.

A request waiting for an authorized decision is different from one waiting for available staff. Treating both as “the team is overloaded” conceals the remedy.

## Look for three specific gaps

**Context:** An operator has to reconstruct the client's situation from several records.

**Authority:** The team can do the work but does not know who may approve an exception.

**Handoff:** The next team receives a task without the required information or a clear acceptance condition.

None of these findings proves that hiring is unnecessary. They identify work the next hire should not have to repeat.

## Give the next operator a usable system

Start with clear ownership, recorded context, and an explicit next action. Use the workflow features already available in your tools. Add a supported integration or focused interface only where it closes a demonstrated gap.

RachelOS shows how a queue and relationship context can give an operator a practical starting point. Its code and interface do not establish a staffing reduction or a universal revenue threshold at which a business changes.

## Make the capacity decision with evidence

After the first change, measure completed work, waiting, rework, and exception load. Decide whether the remaining demand requires additional staff, skills, or another workflow improvement.
