Your team can find the record but still needs you to explain what it means. The client's preferences, unfinished commitments, and exception rules are distributed across your memory and conversations.

The aim is not to make the founder irrelevant. It is to identify which context and authority someone else needs before they can take a responsible next step.

## Record the questions that return

For a week, collect the requests that repeatedly interrupt the principal. Separate missing facts, routine decisions, and genuine exceptions. Record the information used to answer, who has authority, and where the answer belongs afterward.

Do not withhold help to force a process change. Resolve the live request and capture the rule or open question alongside it.

## Build a decision boundary the team can use

Choose one recurring request. Specify the controlling facts, approved action, responsible role, exception criteria, and escalation owner. The principal approves the actual boundary; a sample discount rule from a guide is not business policy.

Test situations where information is incomplete or the request falls outside the boundary. The team needs to know when to stop and ask, as well as when it may proceed.

## Make context survive the conversation

A useful relationship record preserves the current picture, activity history, known facts, unanswered questions, and next commitment. A handoff should not require a new interview with the principal every time.

RachelOS demonstrates that persistent operating context and a next-action view can be built around an actual operator's day. It does not prove that domain expertise is interchangeable or that a fixed percentage of escalations disappears.

## Choose the first fix before a larger build

A new COO or experienced hire may help, especially when decision authority is genuinely being delegated. Give that person the same explicit context and boundaries you would expect a system to enforce.
